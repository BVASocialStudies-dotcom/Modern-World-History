OCTOBER 1904

The Adventures of the Russian Baltic Fleet

Eighteen thousand miles of ocean stand between you and the war. The Tsar has ordered the fleet to sail anyway.

Why Russia is at war with Japan

Russia and Japan both want control of Manchuria and Korea. Russia also needs a warm-water port on the Pacific, so in 1898 it took over Port Arthur in Manchuria.

On February 8, 1904, Japan launched a surprise attack on the Russian fleet at Port Arthur. Tsar Nicholas II expected an easy victory. Instead, Russia's Pacific ships are now trapped there by Admiral Tōgō's navy.

To save them, the Tsar is sending the Baltic Fleet halfway around the world under Admiral Zinovy Rozhestvensky to rescue Port Arthur and defeat the Japanese navy.

The war so far

  1. 1898Russia leases Port Arthur from China and builds a naval base there.
  2. February 8, 1904Japan launches a surprise torpedo attack on the Russian fleet at Port Arthur.
  3. April 13, 1904The Russian flagship Petropavlovsk strikes a mine and sinks. Admiral Makarov, Russia's most respected naval commander, goes down with it.
  4. August 10, 1904The Pacific Squadron tries to break out of Port Arthur and is forced back at the Battle of the Yellow Sea.
  5. October 1904The Baltic Fleet prepares to leave the port of Libau on an 18,000-mile voyage to the war.

Orders for new crew

Sailor,

You have been assigned as a crew member aboard one of the warships of the Second Pacific Squadron. Within days, the fleet will weigh anchor and steam out of the Baltic Sea bound for the Pacific Ocean.

The journey ahead is longer than any battle fleet has ever attempted. Russia has few friendly ports along the way, and the fleet will need to find coal, food, and fresh water wherever it can. You will face storms, heat, sickness, and the constant strain of life below decks.

Departure
Port of Libau, on the Baltic Sea
Destination
The Far East, to relieve Port Arthur
Distance
About 18,000 nautical miles
Commander
Admiral Zinovy Rozhestvensky
Enemy
The Imperial Japanese Navy under Admiral Tōgō

You sail to defend the honor and glory of the Russian Empire. Report to your ship and prepare to ship out.

By order of the Admiralty, St. Petersburg

STAGE 1

Chaos in the Fog

A grinding crash of steel jolts you awake. Alarm bells ring out.

On deck, thick fog hides everything. Shouts pass down the line: the flagship is stuck fast and has to be towed.

Another crash. A cruiser has smashed into one of our battleships. Both must turn back to port for repairs.

Then another ship loses its anchor and must wait for a new one.

The fleet is scattered and short of ships. Everyone thinks the same thing: the Japanese have found us.

But there is no enemy. The fleet did all of this to itself, and you are still in the Gulf of Finland, barely out of Russia's own waters.

What should the fleet do?

The fleet sails on, weaker than before

The fleet steams away without its flagship and several of its warships. Japan's navy is modern, well trained, and waiting. A Russian fleet that arrives short of ships, before it has fired a single shot, has little hope of winning.

Orders arrive from St. Petersburg: the fleet will not face Japan at half strength. Try this round again.

The fleet waits

For days the fleet rides at anchor while crews patch hulls, repair the damaged ships, and bring out a new anchor. It is an embarrassing beginning, and sailors grumble about bad luck. But when the work is done, every ship is back in line, and the fleet is ready to continue its long voyage.

STAGE 2

Shapes in the Dark

It is past midnight. The fleet steams through black water, every gun loaded, every man on edge.

For days, rumors have spread from ship to ship: Japanese torpedo boats have been secretly sent to hunt the fleet and are waiting somewhere in these waters.

A lookout screams. Two small ships have appeared out of the darkness, heading toward the battleships.

Searchlights swing across them. They fly no flag you can make out. They do not answer our signals. They do not turn away.

Torpedo boats are small and fast. One torpedo below the waterline could send a battleship, and every man aboard, to the bottom in minutes.

The gunnery officer grabs your arm. "They're Japanese! If we wait, we're dead!"

The guns are loaded. What do you do?

"Fire, man! What are you waiting for?"

"They're closing in! Remember Port Arthur. They struck first there too!"

Torpedo range in 15 seconds

Open fire!

Every gun in the line roars. Shells tear through the darkness and send up great columns of water around the two small ships. Searchlights blaze across the scene as officers shout over the noise.

No torpedoes come. As the smoke drifts away, the searchlights settle on the two boats, and you see nets hanging from their sides and frightened men on their decks.

What really happened

The two ships were Danish fishing boats. There were no Japanese ships anywhere near, and the fleet was nowhere near the war. It was still in the Baltic Sea, not far from home.

The real Russian fleet opened fire on the two fishing boats. The rumors of Japanese torpedo boats had left the sailors so frightened that harmless fishermen looked like the enemy.

It would not be the last time the fleet's fear got the better of it.

You hold your fire

STAGE 3

Lights on the Water

Word of the fishing boats has spread through the fleet. A few sailors joke about it. Most keep quiet, embarrassed.

Your officer gathers the crew. "No one should be ashamed. The Japanese could be anywhere. Their spies are everywhere, and their torpedo boats could strike from any direction. Better to be too careful than to be at the bottom of the sea."

"Stay alert. The whole fleet is counting on you."

Days later, a frantic message crackles in from one of our supply ships, lagging far behind the fleet: it is being attacked by torpedo boats! Then the message goes silent.

Just after midnight, lights appear ahead. Not two ships this time, but many, small vessels spread across the dark water. A rocket bursts in the sky above them. A signal? To whom?

Some of the small ships are moving toward the battleships. The searchlights catch them. They do not turn away.

The gunnery officer's voice cracks. "It's the torpedo boats the supply ship warned us about! This is the attack!"

"Fire! Don't let them get any closer!"

"The supply ship already told us they're here! What more proof do you need?"

Open fire!

Every gun in the line roars. Searchlights sweep back and forth as shells rain down on the small ships. In the confusion, some Russian gunners begin firing at shapes that turn out to be their own ships. The shooting goes on for many minutes before the order to cease fire is finally obeyed.

What really happened: the Dogger Bank Incident

The small ships were British fishing trawlers from the port of Hull, England. They were fishing on the Dogger Bank, a shallow fishing ground in the North Sea. There were no Japanese torpedo boats anywhere near.

On the night of October 21, 1904, the real Russian fleet opened fire on the trawlers. One trawler was sunk, two fishermen were killed, and others were wounded. In the chaos, Russian ships even fired on each other, and the cruiser Aurora was hit.

Britain was furious, and for a few days the two countries came close to war. British warships shadowed the Russian fleet, and Russia had to agree to an international inquiry and pay the fishermen's families. The rest of the world began to see the Baltic Fleet as dangerous and incompetent.

STAGE 4

The Long Way Around

News of the Dogger Bank spreads around the world. Newspapers everywhere report how the Russian fleet fired on helpless fishermen.

Great Britain is furious. The British control the Suez Canal in Egypt, the shortcut from the Mediterranean Sea to the Indian Ocean. Now they have closed it to the Russian fleet.

Without the canal, there is only one way to reach the Pacific: all the way around Africa.

Our voyage has just doubled in length. Thousands of miles of open ocean, burning heat, and few friendly ports lie ahead. And the officers warn that the Japanese could be waiting anywhere along the way.

Four dangerous encounters lie ahead.

Off the Coast of Morocco

The fleet steams south past Spain toward the coast of North Africa. Everyone is still talking about the Dogger Bank.

At dusk, a lookout spots a steamer with no lights, cutting across the fleet's path.

It does not answer our signals. It does not change course.

"No lights, no flag, no answer. What honest ship sails like that?"

What do you do?

"Fire before it gets close!"

Open fire!

The guns thunder. The steamer swings hard away and disappears into the darkness. No one ever learns whose ship it was, or if it was ever a threat at all.

Off the Coast of West Africa

STAGE 5 · CHOICE 1 OF 2

Madagascar

After fighting through the stormy seas at the tip of Africa, the fleet finally drops anchor off the island of Madagascar.

The ships are almost out of coal. Coal powers every engine in the fleet. Without it, the mightiest battleship is just a floating hunk of steel.

Russia has no coaling stations here. The only people willing to sell coal are German merchants, whose supply ships have been following the fleet.

The Germans know how desperate we are. They are charging double the usual price.

What should the fleet do?

The coal runs out

Weeks later, in the middle of the Indian Ocean, the stokers shovel the last lumps of coal into the furnaces. One by one, the engines fall silent. The fleet drifts helplessly under the blazing sun, thousands of miles from any friendly port and nowhere near the war.

A fleet without coal cannot fight. Try this choice again.

The fleet takes on coal

The German supply ships pull alongside. For days, sailors haul sacks of coal in the burning tropical heat. Black dust coats everything: the decks, the hammocks, the food, the men. Coal is piled everywhere there is space, even in the cabins.

It costs a fortune, but the fleet now has the fuel to reach the war. There is just one problem: new orders say the fleet must wait here for weeks.

STAGE 6

Across the Indian Ocean

The fleet steams east across the Indian Ocean, with thousands of miles of open water in every direction.

The heat is unbearable. Coal is still heaped on the top deck, and every gust of wind blows black dust into the sailors' eyes, mouths, and lungs. Men cough day and night. Some never stop coughing until they die.

Below decks, the animals from Madagascar have gotten loose. Venomous snakes slither through the dark passageways, and sailors are dying from snake bites.

Then the admiral gives an order: gunnery practice. The fleet must learn to shoot straight before it meets the Japanese.

Targets are towed behind the ships. The guns roar. Shell after shell splashes far from the targets.

You fire. A horrible crash echoes across the water. Your shell has slammed into one of our own ships. Smoke pours from its side as it drops out of line.

The damaged ship is falling behind. What should the fleet do?

The fleet waits

The whole fleet sits still in the blazing sun for days. The engines burn precious coal just to stay ready. Food spoils. More men fall sick from the heat and the coal dust, and more are bitten below decks.

Every day the fleet waits is another day for the Japanese to prepare. Back in the Gulf of Finland, waiting made sense. Halfway around the world, the fleet cannot afford it. Try this choice again.

The fleet sails on

It is a hard decision. The damaged ship grows smaller behind you until it disappears over the horizon.

But the fleet cannot stop again. Every day at sea burns more coal, costs more lives, and gives the Japanese more time to get ready. Back in the Gulf of Finland, waiting made sense. Now, halfway around the world, the only choice is to keep moving.

STAGE 7

Japanese Waters

After more than seven months and 18,000 miles, the fleet has finally reached the waters of East Asia.

But the mission it set out on is already lost. Back in Madagascar, terrible news reached the fleet: Port Arthur has fallen. The Japanese army captured it in January, and the Russian ships trapped inside were sunk.

Now the only Russian naval base left in the Pacific is Vladivostok, far to the north. To reach it, the fleet must get past Japan.

The shortest route runs through the Tsushima Strait, the narrow passage between Korea and Japan, right past the Japanese navy.

The admiral must decide: slip through the strait under cover of darkness, or strike at the Japanese at Port Arthur.

Where should the fleet go?

An impossible attack

Port Arthur is now in Japanese hands. Its forts and hills are packed with Japanese guns, and the Russian ships that once defended it lie wrecked at the bottom of the harbor. There is nothing left there to rescue.

The fleet is worn out after seven months at sea. Its ships need repairs, its crews are sick, and its coal is running low. It needs a safe Russian port, not another battle it cannot win. The admiral's orders are clear: reach Vladivostok. Try this choice again.

Into the strait

On the night of May 26, 1905, the fleet steams into the Tsushima Strait. Every warship sails with its lights out. The sea is calm, and a light mist hangs over the water. For a few hours, it seems the plan might work.

But not every ship is dark. The fleet's hospital ships must keep their lights burning, as the rules of war require.

Before dawn, a Japanese scout ship spots those lights. It creeps closer and sees the whole Russian fleet behind them. Its radio crackles out a message: the enemy fleet has been sighted.

By morning, Admiral Tōgō knows exactly where the Russian fleet is, and he is waiting.

The Battle of Tsushima

On May 27 and 28, 1905, the Russian fleet met the Japanese navy in the Tsushima Strait, between Korea and Japan. Tōgō's ships were faster, better maintained, and far more accurate. His crews had spent the months of the Russian voyage training and repairing their ships. The Russian sailors were exhausted, sick, and had barely practiced with their guns.

Tōgō's fleet crossed in front of the Russian line and poured fire into the leading ships. The Russian battleships were destroyed one after another. The admiral was badly wounded and captured.

It was one of the most one-sided naval battles in history. Most of the Russian fleet was sunk, and many of the remaining ships were captured or surrendered. About 5,000 Russian sailors were killed and around 6,000 taken prisoner. Only a handful of ships escaped. Japan lost just three small torpedo boats and about 100 men.

With its navy gone, Russia could not continue the war. In September 1905, the two countries signed the Treaty of Portsmouth, with U.S. President Theodore Roosevelt helping to negotiate peace. Japan won the war. It took control of Port Arthur and southern Sakhalin Island, and Russia recognized Japan's power over Korea.

The world was stunned. For the first time in modern history, an Asian nation had defeated a European great power. In Russia, the humiliating defeat added fuel to the anger that was already boiling over in the Revolution of 1905.

The voyage of the Russian Baltic Fleet is over.
